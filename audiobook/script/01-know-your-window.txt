Chapter One. Know your window.

Every effective system starts with an honest map of the territory. For you, the territory is time, specifically, the arc your medication traces through your day.

Most people who take a stimulant notice three rough phases. First, an onset period, the stretch after taking it when the effect is building but not yet fully present. Then a peak, the hours when focus feels most available, when starting tasks feels almost frictionless and staying with them feels natural. And finally a tail, when the effect gradually fades, and sometimes a comedown, when energy and mood can dip below baseline for a while. The exact timing is different for every person and every formulation, which is why your first assignment is not to take my word for anything. It is to observe yourself.

For the next five days, keep the simplest possible log. Three notes a day. When you took your medication, when you noticed focus was strongest, and when you noticed it fading. That's it. James Clear writes about a similar practice he calls the habits scorecard, where you simply list your daily behaviors and observe them without judgment. The power of the scorecard is awareness. You cannot manage what you have never actually measured, and most of us are running on assumptions about our own days that are years out of date.

After five days, you will see your pattern. Maybe your genuinely great hours are the two to four hours after your morning dose. Maybe there is a reliable dip in the early afternoon. Whatever the shape is, write it down, because that shape is now the skeleton of your schedule.

Here is the principle that changes everything: match the task to the phase. Your peak hours are precious and limited. They should be reserved, fiercely, for what Cal Newport would call deep work, and what you might just call the hard stuff. The project that requires sustained thought. The writing. The studying. The problem that scares you a little. These tasks go in the peak window, and almost nothing else does.

Shallow work, answering messages, tidying, errands, routine chores, gets scheduled in the onset and tail. Not because those tasks don't matter, but because they don't require your best neurochemistry. Spending peak hours on email is like using a blowtorch to warm your coffee. It works, but what a waste of fire.

And here is the trap to watch for. Because the peak makes everything feel engaging, everything will volunteer for your peak hours. Cleaning the kitchen at peak focus feels amazing. Organizing files feels amazing. That feeling is a lie of prioritization. The medication makes whatever you're doing feel worthy of focus, which means the feeling of engagement can no longer be your guide for what deserves it. Your written plan decides what peak hours are for. You make that decision the night before, or in the calm of the early morning, before the window opens, never during it.

One practical rule to close the chapter. Protect the window on your calendar like it's a meeting with someone you deeply respect, because it is. Block it. Name the block after the one task that goes in it. When someone asks for that time, the honest answer is, I'm booked.

Your window is real, it is measurable, and it is yours. In the next chapter, we look at what actually happens inside it, the habit loop, and why the first five minutes matter more than the next two hours.
