Chapter Six. The tail, the comedown, and never missing twice.

Every window closes. In the honest accounting of a medicated day, the afternoon fade and the comedown are not embarrassing footnotes, they are half the story, and a system that pretends they don't exist will collapse the first week it meets reality. So let's design for them directly.

First, reframe what the tail is for. As the medication fades, initiation gets harder again and sustained abstraction gets more expensive. Fighting that with willpower is spending your most limited resource at its lowest ebb. Instead, assign the tail its proper jobs. This is the time for shallow work with built-in structure: answering messages, routine chores, errands, exercise, anything where the next step is obvious and the cost of a wandering mind is low. Momentum matters more than difficulty here, so favor tasks that are inherently sequenced, a list you move down, a room you reset, a walk you take.

Second, give the window itself a closing ritual, just as it had a launch ritual. In the last few minutes of your peak, do one small act of kindness for tomorrow: write a note that says exactly where to resume, what the next step is, and leave the work staged the way chapter three taught. This is your evening stagehand duty done early, while the clarity to do it well is still available. Ending mid-thought with a clear resumption note is a gift; tomorrow's two-minute doorway is literally written on it.

Third, expect the emotional weather of the comedown, and stop treating it as evidence. For many people, the hours when stimulant medication wears off bring a dip, lower energy, lower mood, sometimes a wave of self-criticism about everything the day didn't accomplish. Here is the rule: no verdicts after the window closes. You do not evaluate your day, your project, your worth, or your system during the comedown, for the same reason you don't judge a movie by watching it through a dirty window. If those hours reliably feel bad, or the crash feels severe, that is worth mentioning to your prescriber, that's their department. Your department is scheduling around it: put low-stakes, gentle, ideally social or physical activities there, and let the dip be boring.

Now, the habit science for the days that go wrong, because some will. A morning gets eaten by a crisis. A launch ritual gets skipped. A whole window disappears into a distraction spiral despite everything. James Clear's rule for these moments is one of the most quietly powerful ideas in Atomic Habits: never miss twice. Missing once is an accident, and it does essentially no damage to a habit's long-term trajectory. Missing twice is the beginning of a new habit, the habit of not doing the thing. The goal after a bad day is not a perfect day, it is a not-missed day. Run tomorrow's launch ritual even if today's never happened. Especially if today's never happened.

Attached to that is Clear's warning about the all-or-nothing trap. People with attention difficulties often carry years of practice at self-punishment, and the internal monologue after a lost window can get vicious: the day is ruined, the system doesn't work, I don't work. Clear's answer is that lost days hurt you more by convincing you to abandon the system than by their own cost. One bad window is noise. Quitting the ritual is signal. Track the streak of showing up, mark an X on a calendar for every day you ran the launch sequence, regardless of how the window itself went. What gets measured is showing up, because showing up is the part you control, and, as chapter five established, showing up is the part that everything else follows from.

The window opens, the window closes, and both edges deserve design. What remains is the deepest layer, the person all these windows are slowly building. That is the final chapter.
