Chapter Five. The two-minute doorway.

Here is a paradox you have probably lived. The medication is active. You can feel that focus is available. And yet you still cannot seem to start the one task that matters. You orbit it. You do seventeen small things around it. The window is open and you are standing outside it, and the guilt of that makes starting even harder.

This chapter is about that moment, because habit science has a surprisingly effective answer for it.

James Clear's two-minute rule says: when you start a new habit, scale it down until it takes two minutes or less. Read thirty books becomes read one page. Run three miles becomes put on my running shoes. The insight is that a habit must be established before it can be improved. The version of the behavior that matters most is the version that gets you through the doorway, because the doorway is where nearly all the resistance lives. Clear puts it memorably: a habit that is standardized is a habit that can be optimized. Master the art of showing up, and the rest tends to follow.

Now map this onto your neurochemistry, because the fit is almost uncanny. Your difficulty was never sustaining attention once engaged. On medication especially, engagement sustains itself, that is what the amplified loop does. Your difficulty is initiation, the cold start, the activation energy. Which means the two-minute rule is not a cute trick for you. It is a precision tool aimed at your exact bottleneck. You do not need a strategy for doing two hours of deep work. You need a strategy for doing the first two minutes, because on medication, the first two minutes purchase the next two hours. Once the loop closes on the right task, the medication holds the loop closed for you.

So, for every intimidating task, define its two-minute doorway in advance. Not the task, the doorway. Writing the report becomes: open the document and type one ugly sentence. Studying becomes: read one paragraph aloud. The tax mess becomes: put the folder on the desk and open it. Cleaning becomes: pick up five things. Your only obligation is the doorway. You are formally allowed to stop after two minutes. You almost never will, and you know you almost never will, but the permission is what makes the doorway walkable, because your brain treats one sentence and one report as vastly different sized threats.

There is a companion principle here from the same law of behavior change, make it easy, and its tool, friction. Every step between you and starting is a place where starting can fail. So the doorway must be frictionless: the document already open, from your environment design. The first step already named, on the card from your launch ritual. Reduce the moves between sitting down and being inside the task to as close to zero as possible. And, from the inverse law, add friction to the escape routes. If quitting the task and opening a distraction takes five deliberate steps, the amplified loop you are already inside will usually out-pull the temptation to leave it.

One more tool from Clear's toolkit fits here: temptation bundling, pairing a thing you need to do with a thing you love. Save your favorite coffee for the moment you sit down at the deep work desk, and only that moment. Your favorite playlist plays only during the window. Now the doorway itself carries a small reward, and the craving machinery, remember, currently amplified, starts pulling you toward the work instead of away from it.

The two-minute doorway, staged frictionless, sweetened slightly, sitting right where the window opens. That is the whole move. In the next chapter, we deal with the other side of the arc, the fade, the comedown, and the art of ending a day without losing the plot.
