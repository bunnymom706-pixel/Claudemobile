Chapter Two. The habit loop, amplified.

To steer your focus, you need to understand the machine that produces behavior. In Atomic Habits, James Clear describes every habit as a four-part loop. Cue, craving, response, reward.

The cue is a trigger, something in your environment or your routine that signals an opportunity. The craving is the pull, the motivational charge that makes you want to act. The response is the behavior itself. And the reward is the payoff that teaches your brain, do that again. Every habit you have, good or bad, runs on this loop. Your morning coffee. Checking your phone at a red light. Opening a browser tab the moment a task gets hard. Loop, loop, loop.

Now, here is why this matters so much for you specifically. Adderall works primarily by increasing the availability of dopamine and norepinephrine in your brain. And dopamine, as Clear explains, is not really the chemical of pleasure. It is the chemical of anticipation and pursuit. It is the engine of craving, the part of the loop that says, go get that. Which means that when your medication is active, the craving stage of every habit loop in your life is running hot. Whatever cue you encounter, the pull it generates is stronger. The pursuit is more locked in. The loop closes harder.

This is the scientific reason behind what you have probably felt a hundred times. On medication, you don't just focus better. You lock on. And you lock onto whatever was in front of you when the locking happened.

So the entire game becomes: control the cue. If the craving engine is amplified, then the cue that is present at the moment of amplification effectively chooses your next two hours. The first meaningful action you take as your medication takes effect is not just one action among many. It is the rudder for the whole window. Start on the report, and the report becomes fascinating. Start on a group chat, and the group chat becomes fascinating. Same brain, same chemistry, wildly different day.

Clear has a line that fits here perfectly. Habits are the compound interest of self-improvement. Small choices don't just add up, they multiply, because each one shifts the trajectory of what follows. On medication, the interest rate is higher. The first five minutes of your window compound into the next two hours with astonishing reliability. That can work devastatingly against you, one glance at a feed and the morning evaporates. Or it can work beautifully for you, one deliberate first action and the morning becomes the most productive stretch of your week.

There is a second half to this. Just as the craving is amplified, so is the reward. When you finish a hard task inside your window, the sense of completion lands strongly, and your brain writes a vivid memory: starting hard things during the window feels good. Do it again. Every well-aimed window makes the next one easier to aim, because you are literally training your habit loops under reinforcement. You are not just getting today's work done. You are teaching tomorrow's brain what the window is for.

So the practical takeaways of this chapter are three. First, treat the moment your medication kicks in as the most important decision point of your day, because neurologically, it is. Second, decide the first action of the window in advance, on paper, never in the moment. And third, never open an unplanned input, a feed, an inbox, a chat, during onset. Not because those things are evil, but because you are holding a lit match, and it will catch whatever it touches first.
