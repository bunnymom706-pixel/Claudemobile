Chapter Two. Catch it early. The thirty-second exit.

Every spiral has a moment, early on, when it is still cheap to leave. A thought arrives, the loop starts its first lap, and for about thirty seconds you are standing next to an open exit. Miss it, and the loop starts building momentum, each lap makes the next lap easier, the groove deepens in real time, and an exit that would have cost you a shrug now costs an act of will. Miss it long enough, especially with amplified lock-on, and you are two hours deep with a knotted stomach, wondering where the afternoon went.

So this entire chapter is about one skill, noticing sooner. It is the highest-leverage skill in this whole book, and the science of why comes straight from Atomic Habits. Clear writes that the process of behavior change always starts with awareness, you cannot change a pattern you have not caught running. His most charming tool for this is borrowed from Japanese railway operators, a practice called pointing and calling. Train drivers point at signals and name them out loud, signal is green, and this simple act of naming drops their error rates dramatically, because it drags an automatic process up into consciousness where it can be checked.

You are going to point and call your spirals. The move is exactly this simple. The instant you notice loop-flavored thinking, you name it, out loud if you are alone, silently if not. Spiraling. Or, replay loop. Or, courtroom's in session again. Some people give their loops actual names, the Sunday Dread, the Text Autopsy, the Money Doom. Naming does two jobs at once. It converts you from participant to observer, you cannot narrate a loop and be fully inside it at the same moment. And it interrupts the automaticity, which, for a brain whose loops run amplified, is the difference between grabbing a rope at the cliff edge and grabbing it halfway down.

How do you notice in time, though? You recruit your body, because your body always knows first. Spirals have physical signatures that show up before the story becomes obvious, a tightening jaw, shallow breath, hunched shoulders, a churning stomach, rereading the same paragraph four times, picking up the phone without deciding to. From your chapter one scorecard, you will also know your cue menu, the times, places, and triggers where spirals reliably launch. Those cues now get promoted to tripwires. Sunday evening is a tripwire. That app is a tripwire. Being in bed awake at midnight is a tripwire. When a tripwire trips, you run a one-second check, am I looping? Half the time the answer is yes and you didn't know it yet.

Here is the part specific to your medicated day, and it is worth saying plainly. The focus window cuts both ways. Inside the window, a spiral that gets traction gets amplified traction, which is why chapter three's interrupts have to be physical and fast. But the window's onset, that ramp-up period from the first audiobook, is a special danger zone, because the craving machinery is coming online and looking for a target. This is precisely why the launch ritual from the first book banned unplanned inputs during onset. A cold text read during onset is not just a cold text, it is a cold text handed to an engine that is warming up and hungry. Guard the onset, and you prevent the day's worst spirals before they exist. The best exit ramp is the one you never need.

One more reframe before we move on. Catching yourself mid-spiral is not a failure, it is the win. Every single catch, whether at second ten or hour two, is a repetition of the noticing habit, a vote, in Clear's language, for the identity we are building toward, someone who sees her loops. The catches will come earlier and earlier with practice. That is not a hope, that is just what repetition does to a habit.

You've caught it. You've named it. Now what? You cannot argue with a spiral, and you cannot order it to stop. What you can do is covered in the next chapter, the interrupt.
