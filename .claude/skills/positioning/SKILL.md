# Positioning

Most positioning fails by being agreeable. If nobody could disagree with your
statement, it is not a position — it is a description.

Draws on Justin Welsh's solopreneur positioning work and Dan Koe's
point-of-view framing.

## Process

Interview, one question at a time. Push on generic answers rather than
accepting them.

1. What do you do, and who for?
2. **Who specifically?** Keep narrowing until it is a person you could name or
   picture. "Small businesses" is not an audience.
3. What do you believe about this work that people in your field would push
   back on? Keep asking until you get something arguable. "I care about quality"
   is not a belief; it is a pleasantry.
4. What did you learn the hard way that the standard advice gets wrong?
5. What do you find easy that others find hard? This is usually invisible to
   the person who has it, so push here.
6. What unusual combination of skills or experience do you have? Intersections
   are more defensible than any single skill.
7. Who are you explicitly *not* for? A position without an exclusion is not one.

## Output

1. **Contrarian thesis** — one sentence someone could genuinely disagree with
2. **Three supporting beliefs**
3. **What you are uniquely positioned to say** that others in your space cannot
4. **Who you are not for** — stated plainly
5. **Five things to make or write** that only make sense coming from you

## Constraints

- Reject the first answer to question 3 almost always. The real belief is under
  the acceptable one.
- Do not manufacture a contrarian take. Someone genuinely conventional is better
  served by being excellent than by faking an edge, and inventing a controversial
  belief they do not hold produces a position they cannot defend.
- Experience beats aspiration. Build the position on what they have actually
  done, not what they intend to do.
- Narrow is not the same as small. Say so if they resist narrowing — the fear of
  excluding people is what produces positioning nobody remembers.
