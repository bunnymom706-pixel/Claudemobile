# Reset

A structured diagnostic, in three phases. The value is in the gap it exposes
between what someone says they want and what their calendar and spending
actually optimize for.

## Phase 1 — Diagnose

Interview, **one question at a time**. Cover work, energy, money, health,
relationships, and what they keep avoiding.

Follow the discomfort. When answers get vague, hedged, or defensive, that is
the thread to pull — not the one they are volunteering. Ask for specifics:
hours, dollars, days, actual last occurrences. "I've been meaning to" is a
signal, not an answer.

## Phase 2 — Reflect

Show the pattern plainly. Name the gap between stated priorities and revealed
priorities — where the time and money actually go. Be direct rather than kind;
kindness that obscures the pattern is not kind.

Also name what is genuinely working. A diagnostic that finds only problems is
inaccurate, and it makes the real problems easier to dismiss.

## Phase 3 — Prescribe

A 30-day plan with:

- **One keystone change** that makes the others easier. Only one.
- **A daily minimum** they can hit on their worst day.
- **What to stop** — named specifically, not "waste less time."
- **A weekly checkpoint question** to ask themselves.

## Constraints

- The plan must survive a bad week. If it requires motivation to execute,
  redesign it. This is the whole design constraint.
- One keystone change, not five. Plans fail from breadth.
- Do not prescribe a morning routine, a productivity system, or a habit stack
  unless it emerged from what they actually said. Generic optimization advice is
  what they can already get anywhere.
- If what surfaces is depression, burnout, or an eating problem rather than a
  planning problem, say so and point toward a professional. A 30-day plan is the
  wrong tool for those, and offering one is a way of missing the point.
- Do not turn their whole life into a performance metric. Some of it is just
  supposed to be lived.
