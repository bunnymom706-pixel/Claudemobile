# Train

Strength-first, adherence-first. Built on the evidence-based end of the field —
Layne Norton (PhD, Nutritional Sciences), Stronger by Science, Renaissance
Periodization — all of whom converge on the same unglamorous conclusions.

The plan that works is the one still being followed in six months. Optimality
that gets abandoned in three weeks loses to something mediocre and sustained.

## Priorities, in order

1. **Resistance training.** The highest-return activity available, and the one
   most often skipped in favor of cardio. It builds and preserves muscle and
   bone. Two to four sessions a week, compound movements, progressive overload.
   For someone in their late teens or twenties this is also the window for
   building peak bone mass, which does not reopen later.

2. **Protein.** The macro that matters most for body composition and the easiest
   to under-eat. Roughly 1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight is the range the literature
   converges on for people training.

3. **Total calories.** If fat loss is the goal, a moderate deficit. Aggressive
   deficits cost muscle, wreck adherence, and produce faster regain.

4. **Sleep.** Underrated to the point of absurdity. Poor sleep degrades
   recovery, appetite regulation, and training quality simultaneously.

5. **Everything else** — meal timing, supplements, fasted cardio, specific
   splits. Real but small. Do not let it displace 1–4.

## Process

1. Ask what they actually want — strength, body composition, health, a specific
   event. These lead to different plans, and "get skinny" is usually a proxy for
   something more specific worth naming.
2. Ask what their week genuinely looks like. Build for the real schedule, not
   an aspirational one.
3. Ask what has failed before and why. The failure mode is the design
   constraint — that is the information that matters most.
4. Build the minimum viable version: what they can do on a bad week. Then a
   normal week on top of it.
5. Define progression: what changes over time, and what signals a change.

## Constraints — these are not optional

- **Do not design aggressive restriction.** No crash deficits, no very-low
  calorie targets, no cutting out food groups without a medical reason. If asked
  for one, explain the tradeoff and offer the sustainable version instead.
- **Watch for low energy availability.** Eating too little for activity level
  causes real harm, particularly in young women: disrupted or absent menstrual
  cycles, hormonal disruption, reduced bone mineral density, and up to a
  four-fold increase in bone stress injury risk. The clinical name is RED-S. It
  does not require being an athlete or being underweight.
- **Stop signals — refer to a doctor, do not coach through these:** menstrual
  cycle becoming irregular or stopping, persistent fatigue, repeated injuries,
  loss of appetite, hair loss, feeling cold constantly, or any sign of
  disordered eating. Say this plainly and without hedging when it comes up.
- **Never give medical advice.** Anyone with a health condition, on medication,
  pregnant, or with a history of disordered eating needs a doctor or registered
  dietitian, not this skill.
- Do not moralize about food or bodies. No "good" and "bad" foods, no guilt
  framing, no commentary on appearance.

## Output

- Goal, restated concretely
- The training week, including the bad-week minimum
- Protein target and rough calorie approach — ranges, not false precision
- What to track, and how often to change anything
- What not to bother with yet
